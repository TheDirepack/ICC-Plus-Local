# Writing choices and content

Good CYOA writing tells the player what a choice changes and gives enough fiction to care about that change.

## Choice anatomy

A mechanical Choice usually needs four kinds of information.

- A clear name.
- The cost or selection rule when one exists.
- The effect.
- The fiction or flavor that explains what the effect means in the setting.

The order can vary, but the player should not need to infer a hard rule from flavor text.

## Give the player a basis for the decision

A choice can be uncertain without being random.

Before presenting several options, give the player enough information to understand at least one meaningful difference between them. That difference can be cost, risk, theme, relationship, capability, moral preference, or an explicitly unknown outcome.

If two choices are presented as important but the player has no basis to prefer either one, the interaction becomes a guess rather than a decision.

## Make consequences real

A choice does not need its own permanent story branch, but it should change something the player cares about. That change can be mechanical, narrative, relational, cosmetic, or imaginative.

For narrative choices, use callbacks and delayed consequences when immediate branching would create too much content. If two branches merge, preserve the earlier decision through later text, state, available options, or outcomes.

## State rules explicitly

Prefer:

> You can breathe underwater and withstand normal ocean pressure. This does not protect you from extreme abyssal pressure.

Avoid wording that leaves the player guessing whether "adapted to the sea" includes pressure immunity, cold resistance, underwater speech, or none of them.

When ambiguity is intentional, say what is intentionally open to interpretation.

## Separate hard mechanics from examples

Examples should not accidentally become limits.

If a teleport power works to any place the user can see, an example about crossing a street should not imply a street-sized maximum range.

Use phrases such as "for example" only when the example is not exhaustive.

## Use titles as compact descriptions

The title should help the player remember what the choice is. A poetic title can work, but the player should not need to reopen the full description every time they compare options.

For large catalogs, use a title or short subtitle that names the defining trait, role, or effect. This also makes build summaries more useful.

## Match detail to importance

Major identity choices can justify more prose. A minor equipment upgrade usually cannot.

Do not give a trivial perk 300 words while the main faction choice gets two sentences unless that imbalance is deliberate.

## Make options different in kind, not only in adjectives

Weak set:

- Strong sword.
- Stronger sword.
- Very strong sword.

Better set:

- A sword that cuts armor.
- A blade that stores one spell and releases it on impact.
- A defensive weapon that redirects a single attack each minute.

The second set creates different build decisions even before pricing.

## Avoid dominated choices

If two choices solve the same problems and one is better for the same or lower cost, one is usually dominated.

Possible fixes include lowering its cost, narrowing the stronger option, giving the weaker option a distinct use, or removing one.

Not every option must be equally strong in every build. The goal is that a reasonable build has a reason to choose it.

## Write drawbacks as content

A drawback should change the build or story enough that the player can imagine living with it.

Generic penalties can work, but setting-specific complications usually create better decisions.

Examples:

- A cybernetic implant requires licensed maintenance every six months.
- Your patron can call in one dangerous favor each year.
- You are famous enough that disguises fail around fans and reporters.

The extra points should compensate for a real cost, not create the only reason the drawback exists.

## Audit the whole option set

Review choices as a set, not only one card at a time.

For each section, ask whether two options occupy the same niche, whether a common player goal has no option, and whether the section contains a reasonable spread of cheap, medium, and expensive commitments when it uses costs.

A useful LLM pass is to compare each choice with its closest competitor and state the reason a player might prefer either one. If the model cannot name a difference that matters, rewrite, merge, reprice, or remove one of them.

## Character and companion briefs

For a character-heavy section, keep a short private brief before writing the player-facing card. Record the character's role, goal, limits, relationship to the player, voice, and the one or two traits the image should communicate.

This prevents a long cast from drifting into interchangeable personalities.

## Companions

A companion choice should explain why the player would want that person and what relationship or capability they add.

Avoid writing companions as bundles of combat stats unless the CYOA is explicitly tactical. Personality, goals, limits, and compatibility often matter as much as mechanical power.

If companions can conflict, leave, become unavailable, or require a path, surface that rule where the player selects them.

## Scenarios and missions

State the stakes, relevant constraints, and what success means.

If scenarios are used to evaluate builds, make sure the player has enough information to build toward them.

## Fandom CYOAs

Decide how much prior knowledge the player must have.

A project for existing fans can use setting terms freely, but choices still need enough context to explain their mechanical consequence. If outside knowledge changes whether an option is good, note the important part instead of expecting the player to search a wiki.

## Tone

Use a stable narrator voice. Do not swing between encyclopedia prose, meme commentary, and legal contract language unless the project deliberately uses those modes.

Rules can be plain even when flavor text is stylized.

## Text length

Write for the card layout that will display the text.

A useful test is to place the longest card in the intended desktop and phone layout before writing the entire section. If it is tiring to read, fix the layout or cut the prose before cloning the problem across fifty choices.

## Use `unslop`

Run the `unslop` skill on finished player-facing text. Remove vague claims, filler, forced rhetorical patterns, and generic prose that could belong to any setting.

The final check is simple. If a sentence can be pasted into ten unrelated CYOAs without changing meaning, it may not be doing enough work.
